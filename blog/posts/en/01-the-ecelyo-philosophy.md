# The Philosophy Behind Ecelyo

All complex work shares an uncomfortable feature: ideas keep appearing, while our capacity to execute them remains limited. As a ***project*** grows, the difficulty shifts. It is no longer about generating new initiatives, but about deciding which ones to execute, in what order, and how to keep them from interfering with one another.

Most tools try to solve this problem by offering more flexibility. Ecelyo takes the opposite approach. It deliberately narrows the ***system***'s freedom so that important decisions are made once, and execution can move forward with as little friction as possible.

## A hierarchy oriented towards outcomes

In Ecelyo, work is organised through a stable hierarchy:

***System*** → ***Project*** → ***Tactic*** → ***Task***

The *system* represents the outcome to be delivered. It may be an application, a product, a book, a research project, or any other deliverable.

> [!NOTE]
> **Example**
>
> An application in the Apple ecosystem is still a single *system* even when it is distributed through binaries for iPhone, iPad, macOS, and visionOS, because all of them share the same knowledge base and benefit from the work done across the product.

Within that *system*, a *project* represents a **responsibility domain**. In an organisation, it may align with a team, but that is not its definition. It may also correspond to a platform, a particular application, documentation, or any other functional domain that groups related work.

***Tactics*** are bounded units of **execution**. Each pursues a **single goal** and contains a coherent **sequence** of *tasks*, from an initial *task* that establishes the work to a final *task* that verifies the result. *Tasks*, in turn, represent concrete actions; their meaning depends on the goal of the *tactic* to which they belong.

This relationship is exclusive: a *task* belongs to one *tactic*, a *tactic* to one *project*, and a *project* to one *system*. If a *task* no longer shares its *tactic*'s goal, it should not be moved arbitrarily; the unit of work itself must be reconsidered. **The rigidity is intentional**, because giving up some flexibility reduces ambiguity and makes the *system* easier to understand and maintain.

## Task capture should be free; execution should be disciplined

**Ideas keep appearing**, and they do so in many different contexts: during a conversation with a client, when an error is discovered, while developing a feature, or as the result of a spontaneous thought. Ecelyo does not try to control that creative process. The methodology recognises that capture may happen at any time and from anywhere; the application steps in when an idea enters the *system* and needs to become structured work.

**Execution**, however, **requires discipline**. When work is already in progress, a new idea does not automatically change the priority. Existing work should be finished first, unless there is an explicit reason to change the plan. That way, the freedom to capture ideas does not turn into an endless succession of interruptions.

### Small tactics, cycles that finish

*Tactics* are deliberately small because their **purpose** is not to accumulate work, but to **lead it to a point of closure**. When a *tactic* grows without limit, it stops being a coherent unit of execution and begins to delay the arrival of new ideas into the workflow. Keeping tactics bounded allows the *system* to renew its decision points frequently, so new initiatives do not become trapped behind never-ending *projects*.

## Fewer decisions during execution

One important consequence of this structure is that it reduces the number of decisions required while work is under way. When a human or an agent starts a *task*, they should not have to reconstruct what to do next at every step. Priority, dependencies, *tactic*, and work state already provide that context. **Execution should emerge from the workflow, not from repeated decisions.**

Ecelyo does not try to reduce the number of ideas; **it tries to reduce how long a good idea remains waiting.** To achieve that, it is not enough to structure the work. Its progress must also be ordered. *Tactics* are small, coherent, and sequential by default; **parallelism appears when the structure makes it explicit.** This allows people and agents to move forward at the same time without stepping on one another, coordinating through a shared structure instead of constant conversation.

## Impressions

**This methodology comes from my own experience**, after years of working on genuine epics that took a long time to begin —because they covered too much and everything had to be tied down carefully— and, of course, a long time to finish, if they finished at all rather than remaining half-done. To me, effective productivity only exists when our effort produces something; it does not arise from the process itself. Focus on the tasks: do not procrastinate, but do not try to take on more at once than our brains can handle either.

I have seen dozens of projects, better or worse defined, remain unfinished because of a sudden change in priorities or simply because their definition and execution stretched on long enough to become apparently irrelevant. In software development, the field I know best, unfinished work is not only frustrating; it leaves scars that eventually affect every new idea that follows: more code to maintain, more complexity in future tasks that must preserve both the new product and the unfinished one, and a cognitive burden that grows as team members come and go. They live with the constant uncertainty and fear of breaking products that, without realising it, are unfinished and were never removed.

I do not know whether this happens to you as it does to me: **ideas move through my mind much faster than I can execute them** —let alone compile them— and as soon as I find myself in the middle of a task, I already want to try something new or fix something annoying.

**Ecelyo** is, quite simply, a **methodology** that adds **simple discipline** to my workflow. I can sustain it through an application that acts as a harness, reminding me that **small daily achievements matter more**: always taking one more step forward, and using each small step to improve the direction of travel.

**Something finished, even if it is not optimal, is better than something perfect but only half-done.**
